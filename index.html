Job Market Paper

The Welfare Effects of Providing Information about Externalities
Abstract

Governments routinely use information to motivate prosocial behavior. The welfare effects of doing so depend on how information changes behavior and how it benefits or harms consumers. This paper combines theory and experiments to evaluate three interventions designed to encourage individuals to reduce their carbon emissions: privately revealing the dollar damages from one’s emissions, privately revealing one’s emissions rank, and publicly revealing one’s emissions to another participant. In a model with altruism, social-image, and self-image utility, I decompose the total welfare effect of each intervention into its instrumental value, direct effect on image utility, and external benefits. In experiments with over 6,500 U.S. adults, I estimate these components and find that both forms of private information increase consumer welfare but have no detectable effect on abatement. In contrast, public recognition increases abatement but decreases consumer welfare and total welfare. Evaluating these interventions by behavior alone would reverse their ranking.
