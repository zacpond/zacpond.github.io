Job Market Paper

The Welfare Effects of Providing Information about Externalities
Abstract

This paper combines theory and experiments to study the welfare effects of providing information about personal carbon emissions. I consider three information interventions: impact-based information, which privately reveals the dollar damages from one’s annual emissions; social comparison information, which privately reveals one’s emissions rank among participants; and public recognition, which discloses one’s emissions profile to another participant. In the experiment, consumer welfare effects are measured with a strategy-method elicitation that captures participants’ (possibly negative) willingness to pay for each intervention. Each intervention is then randomized across participants, with abatement effects measured through voluntary contributions to emissions offsets. I find that on average participants have positive willingness to pay for both impact-based information and social comparison information; willingness to pay for public recognition is positive if emissions are low and negative if emissions are high. Only public recognition increases abatement. The private information interventions increase consumer welfare but do not reduce external damages, whereas the public recognition intervention reduces consumer welfare while also reducing external damages. Under reasonable abatement cost curvatures, public recognition reduces total welfare despite having the intended impact on behavior, highlighting the importance of consumer welfare in evaluating non-standard policy interventions.
