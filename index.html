Job Market Paper

The Welfare Effects of Providing Information about Externalities
Abstract

Governments routinely use information to motivate prosocial behavior. The welfare effects of doing so depend on how information changes behavior and how it affects consumers: by improving their decisions or by producing shame or pride. This paper combines theory and experiments to evaluate three interventions designed to encourage individuals to reduce their carbon emissions: privately revealing the dollar damages from one’s emissions, privately revealing one’s emissions rank, and publicly revealing one’s emissions to another participant. In a model with altruism, social-image, and self-image utility, I decompose the total welfare effect of each intervention into its instrumental value, direct effect on image utility, and external benefits. In experiments with over 6,500 U.S. adults, I estimate these components and find that both forms of private information increase consumer welfare but have no detectable effect on abatement. In contrast, public recognition increases abatement but decreases consumer welfare and total welfare. Evaluating these interventions by behavior alone would reverse their ranking.

Simultaneous Confidence Intervals for Partially Identified Parameters Journal of Business and Economic Statistics, 43(1): 232–240, 2025.

Brigham Frandsen, Zachari A. Pond

Abstract

This article extends the Imbens and Manski and Stoye confidence interval for a partially identified scalar parameter to a vector-valued parameter. The proposed method produces uniformly valid simultaneous confidence intervals for each dimension, or, equivalently, a rectangular confidence region that covers points in the identified set with a specified probability. The method applies when asymptotically normal estimates of upper and lower bounds for each dimension are available. The intervals are computationally simple and fast relative to methods based on test inversion or bootstrapped calibration, and do not suffer from the conservativity of projection-based approaches.

Working Papers

The Welfare Effects of Climate Change for Outdoor Recreation

with Jacob Gellman and Matthew Wibbenmeyer

Abstract

Valuation of non-market climate amenities is a crucial component of climate policy. Due to individuals’ direct exposure to ambient temperature, one ideal setting to study these values is outdoor recreation. In this paper, we combine millions of administrative campground reservation records with daily temperature data to study shifts in recreation value under climate change. By exploiting the timing of reservation and cancellation decisions, we estimate how individuals respond to both climate and weather. Preferences for realized temperature follow an inverse U-shape, with disutility from extreme cold outweighing disutility from extreme heat, while reservation decisions respond to the likelihood of cold conditions but not to the likelihood of heat. Climate projections in the western United States predict net welfare gains of $1 billion per year by century’s end, but with substantial seasonal and regional heterogeneity in either gains or losses. Welfare gains depend on individuals’ ability to accurately perceive climatic changes. These findings contribute to a growing set of climate damage estimates.
